This first-party engineering account describes a production research system in which a lead agent assigns parallel investigations, synthesizes results, and passes findings to a citation agent. It reports internal performance and latency improvements alongside substantial token costs, coordination failures, and operational complexity. The account offers relevant architectural observations for delegated research and durable execution, but its reported gains are not independently reproduced or established under matched resource budgets. The article is suitable for reference-only assessment, not as licensed implementation material.

### Method and observed findings

- A lead agent plans research, delegates bounded investigations to subagents with separate contexts, and iterates on their findings before citation processing.
- Development used prompt-and-tool simulations, an initial evaluation set of about 20 representative queries, rubric-based LLM judging, and human testing.
- Production reliability measures included resumable execution, retries, checkpoints, tracing, and gradual traffic migration while retaining older running versions.
- Long-running work used external memory and phase summaries; some subagent artifacts were stored separately and returned through lightweight references.
- The authors report that an Opus 4 lead with Sonnet 4 subagents outperformed single-agent Opus 4 by 90.2% on an internal research evaluation; the supplied account does not establish matched token or tool budgets.
- The account reports typical token use of approximately 15 times ordinary chats for multi-agent systems, compared with approximately four times for agents generally.
- Parallel subagent execution and parallel tool calls reportedly reduced research time by up to 90% on complex queries; this is a source-declared result rather than an independently reproduced measurement.
- The authors report that token usage, tool-call count, and model choice explained 95% of BrowseComp performance variance, with token usage alone explaining 80%; this association does not isolate a causal benefit from delegation.

### Limits and applicability

- This is a first-party engineering report, not a peer-reviewed controlled trial; the supplied evidence lacks sufficient evaluation details, uncertainty estimates, and artifacts to reproduce its quantitative claims.
- The internal evaluation's relative improvement does not establish broader source coverage, complete correctness, or superiority at equal cost; detailed scoring and baseline resource allocation are not supplied.
- The authors identify tightly dependent tasks and shared-context work as weaker fits, and describe synchronous worker batches as a source of blocking and limited coordination.
- Higher token consumption, state-management requirements, and deployment complexity may outweigh benefits outside valuable, substantially parallelizable research tasks.
